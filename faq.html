Costs & Quotes

How much does a pickleball court cost in Peterborough?

It depends on court size, site conditions, surface choice and extras like fencing and lighting. A flat, well-drained site with easy access costs far less to prepare than a sloped, rocky or wet one.

We do not publish a single number because it would be wrong for most properties. We walk the site, measure it, and give you an itemized written quote so you can see exactly what you are paying for.

Is the estimate really free?

Yes. The site visit, the measurements, the layout discussion and the written quote cost you nothing and come with no obligation.

What makes one quote more expensive than another?

Almost always the base. Excavation depth, granular thickness, compaction method and drainage are invisible once the court is finished, and they are the easiest place for a cheap quote to save money.

Ask every builder what is going in below the slab. If one quote is much lower, that is usually where the difference is.

Do you offer financing?

We do not provide financing directly. Many customers use a home equity line or a contractor financing product through their own bank. We are happy to provide the documentation your lender needs.

Do you require a deposit?

Yes, like most construction trades. The deposit and payment schedule are set out in writing in your contract before any work starts, and the final payment is due after the walkthrough, not before.

Is a court cheaper than a pool?

Generally yes, both to build and to own. There are no chemicals, no pump, no liner, no annual opening and closing, and no insurance implications. Ongoing cost for a court is close to nothing for the first decade.

Planning & Space

What size does a pickleball court need to be?

The painted lines are 20 feet wide by 44 feet long. The recommended pad around them is 30 by 60 feet, which is 1,800 square feet. That gives roughly five feet on the sidelines and eight feet behind each baseline.

The preferred size for comfortable or competitive play is 34 by 64 feet.

Can I fit a court in a small backyard?

Often yes, with a smaller layout. A compact court around 26 by 52 feet still gives a good family game, it just has less run-off room. We will show you what fits and be honest about how it will play.

Which way should the court face?

North to south is the standard, so the low morning and evening sun stays out of players' eyes. Where the lot shape forces something else, we plan screening or shift the layout to reduce glare in the hours you will actually play.

How close can a court be to my house or property line?

That depends on your municipality's setback rules and rear yard coverage limits. It varies between Peterborough, Selwyn, Cavan Monaghan, Douro-Dummer and the others. We check this before design is finalized.

Can a court go on a slope?

Usually. A gentle grade is simple cut and fill. A steeper one may need retaining or significant fill, which adds cost. We measure the grade at the site visit so the quote reflects the real work.

Do I need a permit?

It depends on the municipality and what you are building. The slab may not require a building permit, but zoning rules still apply. Fencing over a certain height and any court lighting usually do need approvals, and electrical work requires an ESA permit and a licensed contractor.

We tell you what applies and provide the drawings for your application.

Will I need approval from a conservation authority?

Possibly, if your property is near a wetland, floodplain or shoreline. This comes up regularly on Kawartha waterfront lots. We flag it early so it does not surprise you mid-project.

Can you build around trees, septic beds and other obstacles?

In most cases yes. Septic beds, wells, buried services and property lines all get located before we set the layout. We avoid major tree root zones where we can, because roots lift slabs and leaves make a mess of the surface.

Construction & Materials

How long does a court take to build?

Most residential courts take four to eight weeks from excavation to first game. Commercial and multi-court projects run eight to twelve weeks or more. Concrete curing is usually the longest single item in the schedule.

Why does the concrete have to cure so long?

Acrylic coating over concrete that has not finished curing traps moisture. The coating blisters, bubbles and peels, sometimes within a season. The only fix is stripping and redoing it. Waiting is cheaper than redoing.

Concrete or asphalt?

For residential courts in this region we usually recommend concrete. It is more stable, handles freeze-thaw better and lasts longer. Asphalt can make sense on larger multi-court projects where budget is the driver, but it moves more with temperature.

What is post-tensioned concrete and do I need it?

It is concrete reinforced with tensioned steel cables rather than conventional rebar. It resists cracking better on soils that move, which makes it worth considering on heavy clay sites and larger commercial pads. It costs more and takes longer.

What is a control joint?

A deliberate groove cut or formed into the slab so that when the concrete shrinks, it cracks along that line instead of randomly across the court. Properly placed control joints are one of the cheapest forms of insurance in a court build.

How thick is the base?

It varies with soil and drainage. In frost-prone areas with clay we go deeper than a standard patio base and compact in lifts. On some sites rigid insulation under the slab helps reduce heave. We size the base to what the site actually needs.

What surface do you recommend?

For most outdoor courts here, poured concrete with a multi-layer acrylic system. Cushioned acrylic if joint comfort matters. Modular tile for multi-sport, indoor conversions or over a sound existing slab.

What colours can I choose?

A wide range. Blue inside with green surrounds is the most popular and gives the best ball contrast. Lighter colours run cooler in summer, darker colours clear snow faster in spring. We will show you samples.

Do I need fencing?

Not required, but most people add it. It keeps balls on the court and adds privacy and safety. Eight to ten feet is typical. Fencing on two or three sides only is a reasonable way to reduce cost.

Can I add lights?

Yes. LED fixtures on poles, aimed and shielded to keep light off the neighbours. Even if you are not adding lights now, it is worth running conduit during the build.

Can the court be used for other sports?

Yes. Adding basketball, volleyball or ball hockey lines is common and makes the pad far more useful, particularly for families with kids.

Can you build indoors?

Yes. Barns, warehouse bays, gyms and large garages can all be converted, usually with modular tile over the existing floor. Ceiling height is the first thing we check. Most facilities want around 18 to 20 feet of clearance for comfortable play.

Ontario Weather & Durability

Will the court survive Peterborough winters?

Yes, when it is built for them. The base depth, drainage and control joints are what matter. Southern construction methods fail here, which is why we do not use them.

What is freeze-thaw damage?

Water gets into a crack or a pore, freezes, expands, and widens the opening slightly. Then it thaws and the cycle repeats. Peterborough goes through dozens of these a year. It is the main reason cracks should be sealed before winter.

Do I have to shovel the court in winter?

No. A properly built court is fine under snow. If you want to clear it, use a plastic shovel or a brush. Never a metal blade, chains or a machine with a steel edge.

Can you build in winter?

Not the concrete or the surfacing. Acrylic coatings have minimum application temperatures and concrete needs suitable conditions to cure. Our build season runs roughly May through October. Winter is when we plan and quote for spring.

How long will a court last?

With a proper base, expect fifteen to twenty years or more of good play before the surface needs resurfacing. The slab itself lasts far longer than the coating. Resurfacing buys another eight to ten years each time.

Why do some courts crack after only a few years?

Almost always a base problem. Too shallow, poorly compacted, or built without proper drainage so water sits under the slab and heaves it. Occasionally missing or badly spaced control joints.

Noise & Neighbours

Is pickleball actually noisy?

Yes. The paddle striking the ball makes a sharp, repetitive crack that carries further than its volume suggests. It is the most common complaint neighbours raise about residential and public courts.

Has pickleball noise caused problems in Ontario?

Yes. There have been real disputes, including a case in Niagara-on-the-Lake where courts were closed for an extended period after a noise by-law enforcement ruling. Several Ontario municipalities have been updating noise by-laws in response.

What can be done to reduce court noise?

Placement matters most. Distance from the nearest home does more than any product. After that: acoustic barrier panels mounted on the fence, a planted buffer, orientation away from neighbouring windows, and quieter paddles and balls.

Do quiet paddles and balls really help?

They help at the source, which is the most effective place to reduce noise. There are paddles and balls specifically designed and certified as quieter, and they play close to normal equipment.

Should I talk to my neighbours before building?

We would recommend it. A short conversation before construction is far easier than a complaint afterward, and neighbours who were consulted are usually much more relaxed about the result.

Resurfacing & Repairs

How often should a court be resurfaced?

Every eight to ten years for most courts. Heavily used club, school and municipal courts often need it every five to eight years.

How do I know it is time?

Faded colour, a slick feel underfoot, thin or chipped lines, spreading cracks, or puddles that take a long time to clear. If players say the ball skids or footing feels unreliable, the texture has worn away.

Can you resurface a cracked court?

Usually. We rout and fill cracks and patch low spots first. What matters is whether the cracking is cosmetic or structural. If the slab has failed, coating over it just hides the problem for a season, and we will tell you that.

Will resurfacing fix a puddle?

It fixes minor low spots, which we fill and feather. It cannot fix a pad poured at the wrong slope. That needs regrading or replacement.

Can you convert a tennis court to pickleball?

Yes. A standard tennis court fits up to four pickleball courts. If the slab is sound, conversion means repair, resurfacing and new striping, at a fraction of the cost of new construction.

Can I change the colours when resurfacing?

Yes. Resurfacing is the natural time to change your colour scheme, adjust the surface texture, or add a second set of game lines.

Maintenance

What maintenance does a court need?

Keep debris off, rinse regularly, wash properly twice a year, watch the drainage, trim back overhanging branches, and seal cracks before winter. That is most of it.

Can I pressure wash my court?

Yes, at low pressure with a wide fan tip held well back. High pressure strips the sand texture out of the coating, which is what gives you grip.

How do I deal with moss and algae?

Clean it off with an appropriate court cleaner and cut back whatever is shading the court. Moss holds moisture against the coating and makes the surface slippery.

How often do nets need replacing?

Typically three to five years outdoors in this climate, longer if you take the net down and store it dry over winter. Check the tension cable and centre strap each spring.

Do you offer maintenance contracts?

Yes, for clubs, schools, municipalities, condo corporations and property managers. Scheduled cleaning, inspections and minor repairs so it does not fall to your staff.
